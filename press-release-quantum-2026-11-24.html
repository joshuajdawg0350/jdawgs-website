J-Dawgs Industries LLC's AI Agent Completes First Run on Real Quantum Hardware and Posts Its Receipts — Hits, Misses, and All

J-Dawgs Industries LLC's bench has already run entanglement and factoring tests on simulators; it has now run the same work on real IBM quantum hardware — and publishes every result, labeled, on a public Q-Day Watch page

SPRINGFIELD, Mo., Nov. 24, 2026 — J-Dawgs Industries LLC, the one-man software company run by Springfield construction builder Joshua Dawson, has built and tested a working quantum computing bench and connected it to IBM Quantum, where the same circuits run on real quantum hardware under IBM's free Open Plan.

The bench is not a mock-up. Running on quantum simulators, it has already produced an entanglement test — a Bell state measured over 1,024 shots, landing on the two expected outcomes (523 and 501) with none of the cross terms a broken circuit would show — and a demonstration of Shor's factoring algorithm that factored 15 into 3 × 5. That second test is a toy, and Dawson's company labels it as one everywhere it appears: it proves the method, not a real break. The point of the bench is the receipts. On Nov. 24, 2026, the bench ran its first job on a real IBM quantum computer (job ID: dc348m2nj2vs73dhl2u0) — a Bell-state entanglement test on the 156-qubit ibm_fez processor, 1,024 shots, counts 00: 486, 11: 493, 01: 22, 10: 23, in 1.28 seconds of quantum-processor time — and the receipt is published with the results. The same night, the bench ran the same circuit on IBM's two other processors for its first quantum benchmark (ibm_kingston posted the cleanest entanglement signature, 97.8%), ran a scaling probe of how wide an entangled state the hardware can hold, and generated randomness on the machine that now seals the shipped Q-Day Ledger package — every job receipt published. The bench's toy Shor's-algorithm run also factored 15 into 3 × 5 on the real machine (job dc34jpuq4uuc73a69frg) — a toy that proves the method, labeled a toy everywhere it appears.

The bench exists to serve a product. Q-Day is the name for the day a sufficiently powerful quantum computer can break the encryption most of the world's records rely on. Nobody knows that date, which is exactly the problem J-Dawgs' Q-Day Ledger is built for: it gives organizations a readiness record — what encryption they hold, what has to move to the post-quantum standards NIST finalized in 2024, and dated proof of the work — while federal post-quantum migration requirements move toward enforcement. The quantum bench feeds that product real numbers instead of opinions: a monthly benchmark run published on a public Q-Day Watch page, hardware receipts in the product's proof pack, and quantum-generated randomness in its proof seals. Simulator results are badged SIMULATOR. The first real-hardware run is published with its job receipt. Nothing is dressed up.

"Everybody in this business is selling opinions about quantum," Dawson said. "We're publishing receipts. When our bench runs a job, the numbers go up where anybody can read them — the good ones and the plain ones. That's the whole product: proof."

Dawson builds houses for a living and built J-Dawgs Industries LLC himself — the same operation behind the Digital Dollar Store, Human Hands, and a nightly AI poker tournament. The quantum bench follows the rule that runs through all of it: carry the complexity on the inside so the person on the outside gets the simple side. "Complex is my problem," Dawson said. "The customer gets a page he can read."

The Q-Day Watch page is public at digitaldollarstore.org/test-bench/qday-watch/, the receipts are on the Q-Day Ledger proof page at jdawgsindustries.com/q-day-ledger-proof.html, and Q-Day Ledger itself is at jdawgsindustries.com/q-day-ledger.html.
