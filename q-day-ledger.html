Before any of it can be fixed, one question has to be answered: what cryptography do we actually run — and when must each piece change? Done by hand, that is months of manual inventory work. Q-Day Ledger does it in an afternoon.

What it does

  1. Finds everything. Four scanners inventory your source code, live TLS endpoints, certificates and firmware signatures, and third-party libraries and SBOMs — every finding with the exact evidence attached.
  2. Scores the risk. Every asset receives a documented, reproducible 0–100 score plus a harvest-now-decrypt-later class. No unexplained numbers.
  3. Writes the plan. Each asset mapped to its governing mandate (CNSA 2.0 · EO 14412 · NIST IR 8547 · NSM-10), sequenced into four phases, every line carrying its FIPS 203/204/205 replacement and its deadline.
  4. Packs the proof. One command exports a CycloneDX 1.6 CBOM and an auditor evidence pack, including a procurement questionnaire answered from your own inventory.

Your code never leaves your machine

Q-Day Ledger is local-first, by design. Plain Python 3, zero third-party dependencies. No account, no cloud, no telemetry; nothing phones home. Private keys are recorded by type and size only — key material is never read. For a tool whose job is to inventory your most sensitive systems, that is not a feature bullet. It is the product.

How this could be wrong — stated plainly

Governments have slipped crypto deadlines before; a formal deferral would soften the 2028–2030 demand crest (the need survives; the urgency moves). If the large platforms ship a free, good-enough inventory inside their enterprise stacks, the independent-tool window narrows to the multi-vendor, below-enterprise tier — the tier this tool is built and priced for. The mandate dates are published law and policy: facts. What the market does with them is a prediction, labeled one everywhere it appears, including in our own sales forecast.
