Continuity Craft

The discipline of finding practical knowledge that exists only in people, capturing it faithfully, proving the capture by reproduction, and transmitting it to successors — before the knowledge is lost.

FOUNDED NOVEMBER 8, 2026 · SPRINGFIELD, MISSOURI · BY J-DAWGS INDUSTRIES

Why this field exists

A great share of what keeps the world working has never been written down. It lives in the hands of the machinist who hears a bearing going bad before any gauge does, the farmer who reads a field the way other people read a page, the electrician who knows why this building's panels were wired the way they were. This knowledge is real, it is valuable, and it is perishable. When its holder retires, moves, falls ill, or dies, it does not transfer itself. It simply ends.

Continuity Craft exists so that it does not end.

The method

  1. Survey. Inventory a workplace, farm, shop, crew, or household: what critical work gets done here, and whose knowledge does it depend on?
  2. Prioritize. Score each piece of knowledge for risk — how few hold it, how soon they may leave, how much is written down, what it costs if it vanishes.
  3. Capture. Record one piece of know-how completely: the steps, and the judgment behind them — what the expert watches, feels, and listens for, and how they recover when it goes wrong.
  4. Verify by reproduction. A learner who has never done the task performs it from the record alone, holder absent. Gaps get fixed and the test runs again.
  5. Transmit. Deliver the verified record to its successors, name who now holds it, and set the date it will be refreshed.
The standard

A capture is not a Transmission until reproduction proves it. The field does not claim knowledge was preserved. It demonstrates it — in Verified Transmissions, counted honestly, in numbers a stranger could check.

The founding charter

Purpose

Continuity Craft is founded on one conviction: what humanity knows how to do should not die with the humans who know it. Its everyday work — surveying, capturing, verifying, transmitting — produces a measurable good: capability that still exists in the world after its holder is gone.

Principles

  1. The knowledge belongs to its holder. Capture happens with the holder's consent, in their name, with credit to them.
  2. Proof over claims. Nothing is "preserved" until reproduction proves it. The field would rather report a small true number than a large unverifiable one.
  3. The holder is honored, not mined. The expert is a master handing down a craft, not a resource being extracted.
  4. Plain records, usable by real successors. Written for the person who will actually stand in the holder's place.
  5. Open practice. The method is published, not patented. Anyone may practice Continuity Craft, teach it, and build on it; its founder asks only that its origin be credited and its standard not be diluted.
  6. Do no harm with what is captured. Some knowledge is dangerous, private, or restricted for good reason. The practitioner does not capture what should not circulate.

Practitioners

A Continuity Practitioner is anyone who does this work to the standard: the crew lead recording a retiring foreman's judgment before his last season; the daughter capturing how her father runs the farm's irrigation before the farm changes hands; the nurse documenting what twenty years on the ward taught her. The practice needs no license and no permission. It needs care, patience, and honesty about what the test showed.

If you hold knowledge the world should not lose — or stand near someone who does — you are already in this field's territory.

Take the method. Use the instruments. Make Transmissions. Credit your holders. Report your verified numbers honestly.
